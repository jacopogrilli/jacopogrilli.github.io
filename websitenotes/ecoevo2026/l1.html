Why we write models at all

Ecology and evolution deal with objects — individuals — that are absurdly complicated, in numbers that range from a handful to \(10^{30}\). No model of a population is a faithful description of its parts. A model is useful when it captures a tendency of motion: what happens to a quantity we can measure, under assumptions we can state.

Levins’ point (Levins 1966) is that one cannot simultaneously maximise generality, realism and precision, so model building is the choice of which one to sacrifice. In this course we mostly sacrifice realism and precision: we look for the simplest dynamical rule compatible with a biological mechanism, and we ask what it predicts qualitatively and, when possible, quantitatively.

What we model

We restrict ourselves to systems described by

\[ \frac{d n_i(t)}{dt} = f_i\bigl( n_1(t), \dots, n_S(t) \bigr) , \qquad i=1,\dots,S , \]

where \(n_i\) is the abundance of population \(i\) and \(S\) the number of populations. Writing this down already fixes a lot:

  • the identity of a population is entirely contained in the function \(f_i\) (in the simplest case, in a couple of parameters);
  • there is no explicit time dependence: no seasonality, no changing environment;
  • the dynamics is deterministic: no demographic noise (lifted in L2);
  • there is no space: we do not track \(n_i(\vec x,t)\);
  • individuals of the same species are identical and species do not change: no evolution (lifted in L3).

The focus is not on trajectories but on the long-term behaviour, \(\lim_{t\to\infty} n_i(t)\).

The minimal model: birth and death

Take \(S=1\). Over a short interval \(\Delta t\), the number of individuals changes by births and deaths, each proportional to the number of individuals present:

\[ n(t+\Delta t) = n(t) + \underbrace{b\,\Delta t\, n(t)}_{\text{births}} - \underbrace{d\,\Delta t\, n(t)}_{\text{deaths}} . \]

Here \(b\) and \(d\) are per-capita rates, with units of \(1/\text{time}\): \(b\,\Delta t\) is the probability that a given individual reproduces in \(\Delta t\) (this reading is what we will take seriously in L2). Taking \(\Delta t \to 0\),

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{dn}{dt} = (b-d)\, n = r\, n\ } \qquad n(t) = n(0)\, e^{rt} , \]

with growth rate \(r=b-d\). The population grows if \(r>0\) and declines if \(r<0\). Only the difference \(b-d\) appears: growth is degenerate with respect to the turnover \(b+d\). Demographic noise (L2) breaks this degeneracy.

Two derived quantities are more useful than \(r\) itself:

\[ t_{\text{double}} = \frac{\log 2}{r} , \qquad \text{$e$-folding time} = \frac 1r . \]

Discrete generations

Exponential growth is not an artefact of continuous time. Suppose instead that all individuals reproduce synchronously every \(t_r\), each leaving \(k\) offspring (and then dying — non-overlapping generations). Then

\[ n\bigl((g+1)t_r\bigr) = k\, n(g\,t_r) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n(t=g\,t_r) = n(0)\, k^{\,g} = n(0)\, k^{\,t/t_r} = n(0)\,e^{rt} , \]

so that

\[ \boxed{\ r = \frac{\log k}{t_r}\ } \]

In the ecological literature \(k\) is the basic reproductive number \(R_0\) (expected offspring per individual per generation) and \(t_r\) the generation time \(T\), so \(r=\log R_0 / T\). Note \(R_0 > 1 \iff r > 0\): the two criteria for growth agree, but they are not the same number, and they rank populations differently unless generation times are equal. We will meet this ambiguity again when we ask what fitness is (L8, L14).

Orders of magnitude

\(E.\ coli\) in good conditions: \(r \approx 2\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}\), i.e. a doubling time of \(\log 2/2 \approx 20\) min. Starting from a single cell,

\[ n(6\,\mathrm{h}) = e^{2\cdot 6} \approx 1.6\times 10^{5} , \qquad n(12\,\mathrm{h}) \approx 2.6\times 10^{10} . \]

Humans: the growth rate of the world population is currently \(r\approx 0.015\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}\) (1.5% per year), so

\[ t_{\text{double}} = \frac{\log 2}{0.015\ \mathrm{yr^{-1}}} \approx 46\ \text{years} . \]

What fraction of all humans ever born is alive today?

A calculation that looks like arithmetic and is actually a test of a model. Take the discrete-generation picture, \(n(g) = k^g\), starting from \(n(0)=1\). The number of individuals that ever lived, up to and including the present generation \(g\), is

\[ n_{\text{tot}} = \sum_{h=0}^{g} k^{h} = \frac{k^{\,g+1}-1}{k-1} , \qquad n_{\text{now}} = k^{\,g} . \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{n_{\text{now}}}{n_{\text{tot}}} = \frac{k^{\,g}(k-1)}{k^{\,g+1}-1} \ \xrightarrow[\ g\gg 1\ ]{}\ \boxed{\ \frac{k-1}{k}\ } \]

and, equivalently, for every ancestor who ever lived there are

\[ \frac{n_{\text{now}}}{n_{\text{tot}}-n_{\text{now}}} = \frac{k^{\,g}(k-1)}{k^{\,g}-1} \xrightarrow[\ g\gg1\ ]{} k-1 \]

individuals alive today. The result is independent of the number of generations: under exponential growth a finite fraction of everyone who ever lived is alive right now. With \(k=2\) it would be one half.

Now the data. Estimates put the number of Homo sapiens ever born at \(\approx 117\) billion, against \(\approx 8\) billion alive (PRB), so

\[ \frac{n_{\text{now}}}{n_{\text{tot}}} \approx 0.07 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k \approx 1.07 . \]

Reproduction barely above replacement, averaged over 200,000 years. The current \(r\) is not representative of human history: for almost all of it, growth was nearly flat, and the exponential we measure today is a recent anomaly (see the world population data on Our World in Data). The model is not wrong — it is being applied over a window where its parameters are not constant.

Does anything actually grow exponentially?

Across an enormous range of sizes, generation times and abundances:

size generation time abundance
Bacteria \(10^{-6}\) m \(10^{3\text{–}4}\) s \(10^{9}/\)ml (\(10^{30}\) on Earth)
Mussels \(10^{-1}\) m \(10^{7}\) s \(10^{4}/\mathrm{m^2}\) (\(10^{15}\) on Earth)
Sheep \(10^{0}\) m \(10^{8}\) s (\(10^{9}\) on Earth)

the same minimal model works whenever the population is unchecked:

  • Bacteria. \(E.\ coli\) in a test tube with food: straight line on a log scale, over several decades, until the food runs out.
  • Mussels. Zebra mussels invading the Great Lakes grow exponentially over years, despite sexual reproduction (Jones & Montz 2020).
  • Sheep. Exponential growth with sexual reproduction and small numbers (Flynn & Pereira 2013).
  • Humans. Malthus (1798) made exactly this observation, and drew the conclusion that growth would be checked by war, famine and disease — the Malthusian catastrophe.

When unchecked, virtually any reproducing population grows exponentially. The interesting question is therefore not why exponential but what checks it — which is L4.

Numerics: integrating \(\dot n = f(n)\)

The one equation in this course whose exact solution we know, which makes it the right place to learn how to integrate the ones we do not.

Euler. Expand to first order:

\[ x(t+\Delta t) = x(t) + f\bigl(x(t)\bigr)\Delta t + O(\Delta t^2) . \]

The local error per step is \(O(\Delta t^2)\); over \(t/\Delta t\) steps the global error is \(O(\Delta t)\): first-order accuracy. For \(\dot n = rn\) the scheme gives \(n_{j+1}=(1+r\Delta t)n_j\), i.e. \(n(t) = n(0)(1+r\Delta t)^{t/\Delta t}\), which converges to \(n(0)e^{rt}\) only as \(\Delta t\to0\) — and undershoots it for finite \(\Delta t\).

Runge–Kutta 4. Sample the slope four times inside the step:

\[ \begin{aligned} \kappa_1 &= f\bigl(x(t)\bigr)\Delta t, & \kappa_2 &= f\bigl(x(t)+\tfrac12 \kappa_1\bigr)\Delta t, \\ \kappa_3 &= f\bigl(x(t)+\tfrac12 \kappa_2\bigr)\Delta t, & \kappa_4 &= f\bigl(x(t)+\kappa_3\bigr)\Delta t, \end{aligned} \]

\[ x(t+\Delta t) = x(t) + \tfrac16\bigl(\kappa_1 + 2\kappa_2 + 2\kappa_3 + \kappa_4\bigr) + O(\Delta t^5) , \]

with global error \(O(\Delta t^4)\).

In practice, use scipy.integrate.solve_ivp, which chooses \(\Delta t\) adaptively from an error estimate. The point of coding Euler and RK4 by hand once is to know what the adaptive step is adapting.

Exercises

  1. A population doubles every 20 minutes. How long until a single cell reaches the mass of the Earth (\(6\times10^{24}\) kg, cell mass \(\sim 10^{-15}\) kg)? This is the number that makes “unchecked growth” concrete.
  2. Show that \(r=\log R_0/T\), and construct two populations for which \(R_0\) and \(r\) give opposite rankings.
  3. Two populations grow exponentially with rates \(r_1>r_2\) from \(n_1(0)\ll n_2(0)\). Compute the time at which the first overtakes the second, and show it depends only logarithmically on the initial ratio.
  4. Suppose the per-capita rates are not constant but periodic, \(r(t) = r_0 + A\cos(\omega t)\). Solve exactly and show that the long-run growth rate is \(r_0\): periodic fluctuations average out. (Random fluctuations do not: see bg2025 L1.)
  5. With discrete non-overlapping generations and \(k\) offspring, what fraction of all individuals ever born belongs to the last \(j\) generations?
