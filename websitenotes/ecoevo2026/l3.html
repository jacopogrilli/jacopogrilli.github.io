Lecture 3. Mutations: Luria–Delbrück

Back to index

Biology in ten minutes

Bacteria are attacked by bacteriophages, viruses that bind a receptor on the cell surface, inject their genome and lyse the cell. Some cells survive an otherwise lethal phage challenge: they are resistant, typically because the receptor is altered or missing, and their descendants are resistant too. Resistance is therefore a heritable trait, and one we can score by a yes/no assay: plate the culture with phage, count the colonies.

Antibiotic resistance (L2) is the same story with a different killing agent, which is why the two lectures share their experimental systems. Evolution of resistance can even be watched directly in space, as fronts advancing across a gradient of drug concentration (Baym et al. 2016).

The question in 1943 was not whether resistance exists, but where it comes from:

  • Acquired (Lamarckian): contact with the phage induces resistance in some cells, with some small probability.
  • Spontaneous (Darwinian): mutations to resistance happen at random during growth, before the phage is ever seen; the phage only reveals who already had them.

Both hypotheses predict resistant colonies. Luria and Delbrück’s insight is that they predict completely different fluctuations between replicate cultures — so the experiment is a measurement of a variance (Luria & Delbrück 1943).

Setup

A culture grows exponentially from a single cell for a time \(T\),

\[ n(t) = e^{rt} , \qquad N \equiv n(T) = e^{rT} , \]

with \(N\sim10^{8}\). We then plate it and count the number \(M\) of resistant cells. Repeat over many independent cultures and look at the distribution of \(M\).

Hypothesis A: acquired resistance

Each of the \(N\) cells independently becomes resistant with probability \(\mu_L\) upon challenge. Then \(M\) is binomial, and for \(N\gg1\), \(\mu_L\ll1\) with \(\mu_L N\) finite, Poisson:

\[ P_L(M\mid N) = \binom{N}{M}\mu_L^M (1-\mu_L)^{N-M} \ \simeq\ \frac{(\mu_L N)^M}{M!}\,e^{-\mu_L N} , \]

\[ \boxed{\ \langle M\rangle = \operatorname{var} M = \mu_L N\ } \]

The signature is the Fano factor: \(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle = 1\). The relative fluctuation is small,

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}M}}{\langle M\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_L N}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\langle M\rangle}} , \]

so if the mean is 100 resistant colonies, replicate plates should differ by about 10. The mutation rate is then estimated culture by culture as \(\widehat\mu_L = M/N\).

Hypothesis B: spontaneous mutation

Split the population into sensitive \(s\) and mutant \(m\), with \(s+m=n\). Mutations occur at a rate \(\mu_D\) per division:

\[ \frac{ds}{dt} = r\,(1-\mu_D)\,s , \qquad \frac{dm}{dt} = r\,m + r\,\mu_D\,s . \]

Solving the first, \(s(t) = e^{r(1-\mu_D)t}\), and using \(m = n - s\):

\[ m(t) = e^{rt}\left( 1 - e^{-r\mu_D t}\right) = n(t)\left( 1 - e^{-r\mu_D t}\right) , \]

\[ \boxed{\ \langle M\rangle = N\left(1-e^{-r\mu_D T}\right) \simeq N\,\mu_D\, rT = N\,\mu_D \log N\ } \]

using \(rT=\log N\). Note the extra \(\log N\) relative to hypothesis A: mutants that arose early have been multiplying ever since.

But the mean is not the observable. Inverting it,

\[ \widehat\mu_D = -\frac{1}{rT}\log\left(1-\frac MN\right) \simeq \frac{1}{\log N}\frac MN , \]

would be a legitimate estimator only if \(M\) concentrated around its mean. It does not, and that is the whole point.

The distribution: why it is broad

Exactly, the process is a recursion. Counting births rather than time (no death), when the population is \(n\) with \(m\) mutants the next newborn is a mutant with probability \(p_m = r_m/(r_s+r_m)\), where \(r_s = r(1-\mu_D)s\) and \(r_m = rm + r\mu_D s\), giving

\[ P(m+1\mid n+1) = p_m(m,n)\,P(m\mid n) + p_s(m+1,n)\,P(m+1\mid n) , \qquad p_s(m,n) = (1-\mu_D)\left(1-\frac mn\right) . \]

This can be solved exactly (Lea & Coulson 1949; Gunnarsson, Leder & Foo 2021), but the heuristic below gives the physics in three lines.

When did the first mutant appear? Set \(m=0\) in the recursion:

\[ P(0\mid n+1) = (1-\mu_D)\,P(0\mid n) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(0\mid n) = (1-\mu_D)^n \simeq e^{-\mu_D n} . \]

So the population size \(n_\mu\) at which the first mutation occurs has density

\[ \rho(n_\mu) = P(0\mid n_\mu) - P(0\mid n_\mu+1) \simeq \mu_D\, e^{-\mu_D n_\mu} . \]

How big is its clone at the end? If mutations are rare we can neglect the source term, \(\dot m \simeq rm\), so

\[ \frac{dm}{dn} = \frac{\dot m}{\dot n} \simeq \frac mn \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m = \frac{n}{n_\mu} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{N}{n_\mu} . \]

A mutation that happens when the culture is small produces a jackpot; one that happens at the last division contributes a single cell.

Change variables \(n_\mu = N/M\), \(|dn_\mu/dM| = N/M^2\):

\[ \boxed{\ P(M\mid N) \simeq \frac{N\mu_D}{M^{2}}\;e^{-\mu_D N/M} \ \simeq\ \frac{N\mu_D}{M^{2}}\quad (M \gg \mu_D N)\ } \]

or in terms of the frequency \(X=M/N\), \(P(X)\simeq \mu_D X^{-2}e^{-\mu_D/X}\). A power-law tail with exponent 2, cut off at \(M\sim N\) because a clone cannot be bigger than the culture, and suppressed below \(M\sim \mu_D N\) because the first mutation cannot be arbitrarily late.

Moments

The tail controls everything. With the cut-offs \(1\le M\le N\):

\[ \langle M\rangle = \int_1^N \! dM\; M\,P(M\mid N) \simeq N\mu_D \log N , \qquad \langle M^2\rangle = \int_1^N \! dM\; M^2\,P(M\mid N) \simeq N^{2}\mu_D , \]

so that the squared coefficient of variation is

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{\operatorname{var}M}{\langle M\rangle^{2}} = \frac{\langle M^2\rangle}{\langle M\rangle^2}-1 \simeq \frac{1}{\mu_D \log^2 N} - 1 \ } \]

Compare the two hypotheses at equal mean:

acquired spontaneous
distribution Poisson \(\sim N\mu/M^2\)
\(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle\) \(1\) \(\sim N/\log N\)
\(\mathrm{CV}\) \(1/\sqrt{\langle M\rangle}\) \(\sqrt{N/(\langle M\rangle \log N)}\)

The spontaneous hypothesis has a CV larger by a factor \(\sqrt{N}\) — eight orders of magnitude in \(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle\) for \(N=10^8\). This is not a subtle statistical test. This is what Luria and Delbrück measured, and the answer was: wildly fluctuating, with occasional jackpot plates. Mutations are spontaneous.

Where the heuristic breaks

Keeping only the descendants of the first mutant, the same integral gives \(\langle M\rangle \simeq N\mu_D\log(1/\mu_D)\), not \(N\mu_D\log N\). The two agree only when \(\mu_D N = O(1)\), i.e. when there really is about one mutational event per culture. For \(\mu_D N \gg 1\) the later mutations matter and the exact treatment is needed — the tail exponent, however, is unchanged.

The exact structure: a compound Poisson

The heuristic generalises into the exact answer with the tools of L2. Mutational events are a Poisson process along divisions: the number of events in a culture grown to \(N\) cells is Poisson with mean

\[ m_{\text{ev}} = \mu_D N . \]

An event occurring at population size \(n\) founds a clone that grows to \(j=N/n\) cells. Since \(n\) is uniform on \([1,N]\), the clone size has density

\[ P(j) = \frac{1}{j^{2}} \quad (j\ge1) , \]

and the total number of mutants is a sum of a Poisson number of such clones — a compound Poisson. Its pgf is therefore \(G(z) = \exp\{ m_{\text{ev}}[\phi(z)-1]\}\), with \(\phi\) the pgf of the clone size. The discrete version of the clone-size law is \(P(j)=1/[j(j+1)]\), for which

\[ \phi(z) = \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{z^{j}}{j(j+1)} = 1 + \frac{1-z}{z}\log(1-z) , \]

giving the Lea–Coulson generating function

\[ \boxed{\ G(z) = \exp\left[ m_{\text{ev}}\,\frac{1-z}{z}\,\log(1-z) \right]\ } \]

Everything follows from it:

  • \(P(M=0) = G(0) = e^{-m_{\text{ev}}}\) — the fraction of cultures with no resistant cell;
  • \(\log G\) has a logarithmic singularity at \(z=1\): the mean diverges, and is finite only because the culture is finite. Cutting off at \(j\le N\) restores \(\langle M\rangle = m_{\text{ev}}\log N\);
  • the \(z\to1\) singularity of the pgf is equivalent to the \(1/M^2\) tail of the distribution.

How to measure a mutation rate from jackpots

Since \(\langle M\rangle\) is dominated by rare plates, the sample mean is a terrible estimator: it has enormous variance and is strongly biased downward in small experiments. Three better options, in increasing order of efficiency:

1. The \(p_0\) method. Count only the fraction of cultures with zero resistant colonies. That is a Poisson probability, and nothing else enters:

\[ p_0 = e^{-\mu_D N} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ \widehat\mu_D = -\frac{\log p_0}{N}\ } \]

Robust, immune to the tail, and useless if \(p_0\) is near 0 or 1 — so the experiment must be designed with \(\mu_D N \sim 1\).

2. Maximum likelihood. Invert \(G(z)\) numerically for \(P(M\mid N)\) and maximise the likelihood over \(\mu_D\). The practical recipe is the discrete convolution of Ma, Sandri & Sarkar 1992, used by all modern fluctuation-test software.

3. Median-based estimators. The median of the distribution is well behaved even when the mean is not (Lea & Coulson’s method).

The general lesson travels far beyond bacteria: when a distribution has a heavy tail, estimate its parameters from the bulk or from the probability of zero, not from the mean.

Where else the same calculation shows up

  • Sequencing instead of plating. A single clonal expansion carries, in its site frequency spectrum, a \(1/f^{2}\) tail with exactly the same origin: mutations that arise early are carried by many descendants. One can read mutation rates off a single deep-sequenced population (Gunnarsson, Leder & Foo 2021; Pompei et al. 2025).
  • Macroevolution. The same “the earlier you appear, the bigger you get” logic, applied to genera instead of clones, gives Yule’s law for the number of species per genus (Yule 1925) — a power law from a birth process, no selection needed. A preview of L21.

Notebook

  1. Simulate the fluctuation test: stochastic exponential growth from one cell with mutation at rate \(\mu_D\) per division, scored at \(N\). Repeat \(10^3\) times.
  2. Plot the survival function \(P(M>x)\) on log–log axes and read off the exponent \(-1\) (i.e. \(P(M)\sim M^{-2}\)).
  3. Simulate the Lamarckian null (binomial at the end) and compare the two histograms at the same mean. Compute \(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle\) for both.
  4. Estimate \(\mu_D\) with the \(p_0\) method and by maximum likelihood; compare their bias and spread as a function of the number of cultures.
  5. Data. Luria & Delbrück’s original tables (1943): apply the estimators and compare with the published numbers.

Exercises

  1. Show that the Poisson limit of the binomial requires only \(N\to\infty\), \(\mu_L\to0\) with \(\mu_L N\) fixed, and estimate the error for \(N=10^8\), \(\mu_L=10^{-8}\).
  2. Derive \(\phi(z)=1+\frac{1-z}{z}\log(1-z)\) from \(P(j)=1/[j(j+1)]\), and verify \(G(0)=e^{-m_{\text{ev}}}\).
  3. Work out \(\langle M\rangle\) and \(\langle M^2\rangle\) with explicit cut-offs and show which cut-off dominates each.
  4. Suppose mutants grow with rate \(r'\ne r\). Show that the clone-size exponent becomes \(1+r/r'\), so that the tail is a measurement of the mutant’s fitness cost.
  5. Design a fluctuation test: given \(\mu_D\approx10^{-8}\) and \(N\approx10^{8}\), how many cultures are needed to estimate \(\mu_D\) within 10% by the \(p_0\) method?
  6. Why does the Lamarckian hypothesis predict a Fano factor of exactly 1, while any history-dependent mechanism predicts more? (This is the general logic of a fluctuation test.)
