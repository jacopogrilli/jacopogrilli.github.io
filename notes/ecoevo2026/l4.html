Lecture 4. Limits to growth: finite resources

In L1 we looked at these curves over the window in which they are straight on a log scale: E. coli in a test tube, zebra mussels in the Great Lakes, sheeps. If we look over longer timescales, each of them eventually stops growing.

What stops the growth?

Malthus

Malthus’s (1798): unchecked, a population grows geometrically (=exponentially), while subsistence grows at best arithmetically (=lineary), so growth must be limited. Stated this way it is about rates, and it already contains an ambiguity we will have to take into account:
- resources can be a flux — something added so much per year, forever.
- the resource can be a stock, provided in batch: e.g., a finite amount of sugar in a test tube, to be consumed once

Those are different models/settings.
First, an effective description that avoid this distinction

Logistic growth as an effective model

Verhulst’s answer (1838) is phenomenological. Do not specify what the limiting factor is, just require that the per-capita growth rate decreases with density. We want to modify
\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = N \left(r - \gamma(N) \right) \]
where \(\gamma(N)\) increases with \(N\)
Simplest choice \(\gamma(N) \sim N\)

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = r N \left(1-\frac{N}{N_{\max}}\right) \]
can be solved exactly by separating variables (and partial fractions, \(\frac{1}{N(1-N/N_{\max})} = \frac{1}{N} + \frac{1}{N_{\max}-N}\))
\[ \frac{dN}{N(1-N/N_{\max})} = r dt \]
integrating from \(N(0)\) to N we get
\[ N(t) = \frac{N_0 N_{\max} e^{rt}}{(N_{\max}-N_0) + N_0 e^{rt}} \]

\(r\) is the growth rate of L1, measured at low density, and \(N_{\max}\) — the carrying capacity — is whatever density makes the growth stop.

It is conveninent to make the equation adimentional, by measuring time in units of \(1/r\) and abundances in units of \(N_\max\)
Introducing \(u=N/N_{\max}\) and \(\tau=rt\), the model has no free parameters
\[\frac{du}{d\tau} = u(1-u)\]

Graphical method for long-term behavior

For large times, if \(N(0)>0\), \(N \to N_\max\), while if \(N(0)=0\) it will be zero forever. We do not need to solve exactly the model for all times to arrive to this conclusion

Plot the right-hand side \(dN/dt = f(N) =rN(1-N/N_{\max})\) against \(N\). A parabola with \(f(N)=0\), in \(N=0\) and \(N=N_{\max}\). Where \(dN/dt>0\) the population increases, where \(dN/dt<0\) decreases, so the arrows on the \(N\) axis point away from \(0\) and towards \(N_{\max}\):

It might be useful to introduce \(dN/dt=N\,g(N)\), where \(g(N)\) is the percapita growth rate.
Plot is a straight line hitting zero at \(N = N_\max\)

Fixed point and their stability

The two points where \(f\) vanishes are fixed points: put the population exactly there and it stays there for ever, since \(dN/dt=0\). Whether it stays there when it is perturbed (stability) is a different question.

Write \(N(t) = N^* + \delta(t)\) with \(\delta\) small and expand, using \(f(N^*)=0\):

\[ \frac{d\delta}{dt} = f(N^*+\delta) = \underbrace{f(N^*)}_{=\,0} + f'(N^*)\,\delta + O(\delta^2) \simeq f'(N^*)\,\delta , \]
if we keep the first order only \(\delta(t) = \delta(0)\,e^{f'(N^*)t}\). So the sign of one number decides:

  • \(f'(N^*)<0\): perturbations decay, the fixed point is stable, and \(1/|f'(N^*)|\) is the time the population takes to come back;
  • \(f'(N^*)>0\): perturbations grow, the fixed point is unstable — the population leaves, and the linearization is only good while \(\delta\) stays small.

If \(f'(N^*)=0\) the linear term decides nothing and one has to look at the next order; that borderline is where the bifurcations of L5 live. For the logistic:

  • \(N^*=0\) is a fixed point, and \(f'(0)=r>0\) makes it unstable;
  • \(N^*=N_{\max}\) is a fixed point, and \(f'(N_{\max})=-r<0\) makes it stable: any perturbation decays at rate \(r\).

The trajectories follow: from \(0<N(0)<N_{\max}\) a sigmoid, steepest where \(f\) is largest, at \(N=N_{\max}/2\); from \(N_0>N_{\max}\) a monotone decay onto \(N_{\max}\).
This is very general: nothing here used the logistic form, what matters are signs of the derivatives evaluated in the fixed point

Growth on limited resources

The logistic says that something limits growth but does not specify what it is. Now let’s consider resources explicitly.

We need to introduce two parameters:
- the yield \(Y\), the number of cells made per unit of resource (the conversion from resource to biomass)
- \(\phi(R)\) the fraction of the maximum growth rate reached at concentration \(R\).

Why growth saturates

What should \(\phi(R)\) look like? A cell does not eat the medium directly: each resource molecule has to be imported by a transporter in the membrane and processed by an enzyme. Say a cell has \(E\) transporters / enzymes. Each one is either free, waiting for a molecule, or busy, holding one:

\[ E_{\text{free}} + R \;\underset{k_-}{\overset{k_+}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; E_{\text{busy}} \;\xrightarrow{\;k_{\text{cat}}\;}\; E_{\text{free}} + \text{resource inside the cell} . \]

\(E_{\text{busy}}\) can be thought as a bound state between \(E\) and \(R\)

A free transporter/enzyme binds a molecule at rate \(k_+R\) (mass action: encounters are proportional to the concentration), lets it go at rate \(k_-\), and imports it at rate \(k_{\text{cat}}\). The number of busy transporters \(E_{\text{busy}}\) obeys

\[ \frac{dE_{\text{busy}}}{dt} = k_+R\,(E-E_{\text{busy}}) - (k_- + k_{\text{cat}})\,E_{\text{busy}} . \]

These are molecular rates, fractions of a second, while the cell grows over hours. So \(E_{\text{busy}}\) is always at its quasi-steady state (adiabatic approximation)

\[ E_{\text{busy}}^* = E\,\frac{R}{K_R+R} , \qquad K_R = \frac{k_- + k_{\text{cat}}}{k_+} . \]

The import flux per cell is then

\[ J(R) = k_{\text{cat}}\,E_{\text{busy}}^* = J_{\max}\,\frac{R}{K_R+R} , \qquad J_{\max} = k_{\text{cat}}E . \]

Now assume this is the only limiting resource (everything else is in excess), so that a cell divides each time it has imported \(1/Y\) units of it. The per-capita growth rate is then \(Y J(R)\). Writing \(b = YJ_{\max}\) for the rate when the resource is in excess, \(b\,\phi(R) = Y J(R)\), which gives the hyperbola below. This is Michaelis–Menten enzyme kinetics (1913) applied to uptake.

The two regimes:
- At low \(R\), the transporters/enzymes are mostly free and waiting, so growth is limited by encounters, \(\propto R\).
- At high \(R\), they are all busy, so growth is limited by the cell’s handling capacity, independent of \(R\).

We can think at this mechanism a budget for limited time. With \(k_-=0\), a transporter alternates between waiting, on average \(1/(k_+R)\), and importing, \(1/k_{\text{cat}}\). So it imports \(1/\bigl(1/(k_+R)+1/k_{\text{cat}}\bigr)\) molecules per unit time. What produces the shape is a finite machinery that spends a fixed time on each process.

Monod measured in 1949 the growth rate of bacteria on different concentrations

\[ \phi(R) = \frac{R}{K_R+R} , \]
(as Monod himself noticed is surprising that this applies to the growth rate as the growth rate is determined by a complex combination of factors)

It vanishes at \(R=0\) (no food, no growth)
it saturates at \(\phi=1\) (with food in excess, growth is limited by the cell, not by the medium)
and it is at half of that when \(R=K_R\), the half-saturation constant, which measures the affinity of the cells for the resource.

What matters below are the two regimes it separates — \(\phi\simeq R/K_R\), linear, for \(R\ll K_R\), and \(\phi\simeq1\), constant, for \(R\gg K_R\) — and those are quite generic: any \(\phi\) that starts at zero, increases and saturates gives the same two limits.

Note that \(\phi\) is a function of the resource concentration, while the logistic is a statement about the population density. What turns one into the other is the resource dynamics, and it does so differently in the two cases below.

A closed environment: a batch culture

Now the case of the curves of the first section: a test tube, where the resource is a stock to be consumed once and nothing is supplied. Growing populations convert resources into biomass, so

\[ \frac{dN}{dt}= b N \phi(R) , \qquad \frac{dR}{dt} = - \frac{1}{Y} b \phi(R) N = - \frac{1}{Y} \frac{dN}{dt} \]

Deaths are neglected here: during the growth phase cells divide and do not die, which is what makes the second equation exact. The two equations say that resource is not lost, only converted, so

\[ R + \frac{N}{Y} = R_{\text{tot}} = \text{constant} , \qquad N_{\max} = Y R_{\text{tot}} , \]
then \(R = (N_\max-N)/Y\)
\(N_{\max}\) being the population reached when the resource is gone. This single conservation law eliminates \(R\) and leaves one equation,

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{dN}{dt} = b\,N\,\phi((N_\max-N)/Y) = b\,N\,\frac{N_{\max}-N}{K_R Y + N_{\max}-N}\ } \]

whose shape is controlled by one dimensionless number, \(\kappa = K_R Y/N_{\max} = K_R/R_{\text{tot}}\): the affinity of the cells for the resource, measured against how much resource there is.

  • \(\kappa\gg1\) (\(R_{\text{tot}} \ll K_R\)) — the resource is always scarce compared with \(K_R\), so \(\phi\) is in its linear regime. Then \(K_RY\) dominates the denominator and
    \[ \frac{dN}{dt} \simeq \frac{b}{\kappa}\,N\left(1-\frac{N}{N_{\max}}\right) , \]
    exactly Verhulst, with growth rate \(bR_{\text{tot}}/K_R\) and carrying capacity \(N_{\max}=YR_{\text{tot}}\). The logistic is the unsaturated limit of a consumer and its resource, and its \(N_{\max}\) is a property of the environment (how much food) and of the conversion (the yield), not of the organism alone.
  • \(\kappa\ll1\) (\(R_{\text{tot}} \gg K_R\)) — the medium is saturating, \(\phi\simeq1\), and
    \[ \frac{dN}{dt} \simeq b\,N \]
    until the last fraction \(\kappa\) of the resource is left, when growth collapses within about one doubling.

The per-capita rate makes the contrast visible: \(\dot N/N\) falls linearly with \(N\) in the first case and stays flat until the very end in the second. Real bacterial batch cultures are often at \(\kappa\ll1\) and this is why the logistic is a poor description of them, while sigmoids with a sharper knee fit better (Zwietering et al. 1990).

An open environment: a supply of resource

Let’s consider Malthus’ quote
unchecked, a population grows geometrically, while subsistence grows at best arithmetically

Let’s assume that a resource is supplied at a constant rate \(s\) — the “arithmetic” growth. Population grow on a resource (births) and die with rate \(d\) independent of the resource

\[ \frac{dN}{dt}= (b \phi(R) - d) N \]
\[ \frac{dR}{dt} = s - \frac{1}{Y} b \phi(R) N \]

if the dynamics of resources is faster. Adiabatic approximation
\(dR/dt \approx 0\)
than resources are “at equilibrium” with the population
\[ \phi(R^*) = \frac{s Y}{b N} \]
then we get
\[ \frac{dN}{dt} \approx s Y - d N \]

When is this allowed? Only where the equilibrium exists. Since \(\phi\le1\), the condition \(\phi(R^*)=sY/(bN)\le1\) means

\[ N \ \ge\ N_c \equiv \frac{sY}{b} , \]

the population large enough to consume the whole supply. Below \(N_c\) there is no such balance: the resource accumulates, \(\phi\to1\), and the population grows exponentially as in L1. The two regimes are

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} \simeq (b-d)\,N \quad (N<N_c) , \qquad \frac{dN}{dt} \simeq sY - dN \quad (N>N_c) , \]

at \(N=N_c\) both give \(\dot N = sY\,(1-d/b)\).

After long times, the population approaches

\[ \boxed{\ N^* = \frac{sY}{d}\ } \]

approached at rate \(d\), since \(\dot N = sY-dN\) is linear: \(N(t) = N^* + (N_0-N^*)e^{-dt}\).
Note that \(N^* > N_c\) whenever \(b>d\), i.e. whenever the population grows when resources are abundant

What a carrying capacity is

open (supply \(s\)) closed (batch)
what limits a flux of resource a stock of resource
ceiling \(N_{\max}=sY/d\) \(N_{\max}=Y R_{\text{tot}}\)
approach to it exponential, at rate \(d\) over one doubling (\(\kappa\ll1\)) or as a sigmoid (\(\kappa\gg1\))
relaxation rate after a perturbation \(d\) \(r\) (logistic limit)

So the carrying capacity is not a property of a species: it is a property of the species and its environment.

Why does the logistic keep working then? Because it is the generic behaviour near a ceiling. Take any \(\dot N = N g(N)\) with a stable fixed point, \(g(N^*)=0\) and \(g'(N^*)<0\), and expand:

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} \simeq -g'(N^*)\,N^*\;N\left(1-\frac{N}{N^*}\right) , \]

the logistic, with an effective \(r=-g'(N^*)N^*\). Any mechanism looks logistic close enough to its ceiling; the mechanisms differ far from it, in the early growth — which is exactly the part of the curve a batch experiment measures well.

Exctinction

Everything so far has been about where a population settles. What happens if we change parameters? For instance we could increase mortality (e.g. add a drug that kills at rate \(\delta\)). In the logistic, mortality enters as \(-\delta N\):

\[ \frac{dN}{dt} = r N\left(1-\frac{N}{N_{\max}}\right) - \delta N = N\left[\,(r-\delta) - \frac{r}{N_{\max}}\,N \right] , \]

with fixed points \(N^*=0\) and \(N^*_+ = N_{\max}\left(1-\delta/r\right)\), and slopes \(f'(0)=r-\delta\) and \(f'(N^*_+)=-(r-\delta)\): opposite signs, so the two exchange stability at \(\delta_c=r\): a transcritical bifurcation, with the ceiling falling linearly to zero. Graphically it is the picture of the graphical method, with the per-capita line pushed down by \(\delta\) until it no longer crosses zero.

The extinction is continuous. The ceiling goes to zero as the threshold is approached, so the population is driven down gradually and a small reduction of the mortality brings it back.

Exercises

  1. Nondimensionalize the logistic with \(u=N/N_{\max}\), \(\tau=rt\), and show that the solution depends only on \(u_0\).
  2. Show that the logistic grows fastest at \(N=N_{\max}/2\), where it produces biomass at rate \(rN_{\max}/4\). (This is where the “maximum sustainable yield” of fisheries comes from, and L5 will show what is wrong with harvesting there.)
  3. Batch culture: derive the conservation law and the single equation above, take the limits \(\kappa\gg1\) and \(\kappa\ll1\), and sketch \(\dot N/N\) against \(N\) in both.
  4. Check that the two regimes of the open model match at \(N_c=sY/b\), and find where the supply-limited branch would wrongly predict infinite per-capita growth.
  5. Integrate the open model numerically without the adiabatic approximation, for \(N\) below and above \(N_c\), and compare with the two regimes. How fast must the resource be for the approximation to hold?
  6. For a general \(\dot N = N g(N)\) with a stable \(N^*\), derive the local logistic above and identify the effective \(r\). Apply it to the supply-limited model and recover the relaxation rate \(d\).
