Lecture 3. Mutations: Luria–Delbrück

Biology intro

Some cells survive a dose of antibiotic that kills the rest of the culture: they are resistant, typically because the target of the drug, or its uptake, is altered. It is the system of L2. Evolution of resistance can even be watched directly in space, as fronts advancing across a gradient of drug concentration (Baym et al. 2016).

In principle resistance could be:
- Acquired (Lamarckian): contact with the drug induces resistance in some cells, with some small probability.
- Spontaneous (Darwinian): mutations to resistance happen at random during growth, before the drug is ever met; the drug only reveals who already had them.

Both hypotheses predict resistant colonies. In 1943, Luria and Delbrück’s insight is that they predict completely different fluctuations between replicate cultures — so the experiment is a measurement of a variance (Luria & Delbrück 1943).

The 1943 experiment used a different killing agent, bacteriophages: viruses that bind a receptor on the cell surface, inject their genome and lyse the cell (killing it). Resistance is usually an altered or missing receptor. For what we care about here the two are equivalent: a challenge that kills the cells. Below we’ll refer to antibiotics.

Setup

A culture grows exponentially (without antibiotics) from a single cell for a time \(T\),

\[ n(t) = e^{rt} , \qquad N \equiv n(T) = e^{rT} , \]

with \(N\sim10^{8}\).
Plate the culture with antibiotics (or phages as in the original experiment) and count the number \(M\) of resistant cells. Repeat over many independent cultures and look at the distribution of \(M\).

Usually only a fraction \(\varepsilon\) of the culture is plated, which thins \(M\) and leaves the shape of its tail unchanged, but does change the probability of an empty plate, to be corrected for \(\varepsilon\) before it is used to estimate \(\mu\) (Stewart 1991; formula in the appendix).

Hypothesis A: acquired resistance (Lamarckian)

Each of the \(N\) cells independently becomes resistant with probability \(\mu_L\) upon challenge (antibiotics, phages,…).
Then \(M\) is binomial, and for \(N\gg1\), \(\mu_L\ll1\) with \(\mu_L N\) finite, Poisson:

\[ P_L(M\mid N) = \binom{N}{M}\mu_L^M (1-\mu_L)^{N-M} \ \simeq\ \frac{(\mu_L N)^M}{M!}\,e^{-\mu_L N} , \]
The expected number of resistant colonies is
\[ \boxed{\ \langle M\rangle = \mu_L N\ } \]
The variance of a Poisson is equal to the mean, resulting in the Fano factor \(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle = 1\). The relative fluctuation (the coefficient of variation) is small,

\[ \mathrm{CV} = \frac{\sqrt{\operatorname{var}M}}{\langle M\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_L N}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\langle M\rangle}} , \]

so if the mean is 100 resistant colonies, replicate plates should differ by about 10.
The mutation rate is then estimated as \(\widehat\mu_L = M/N\).

Hypothesis B: spontaneous mutation

Split the population into sensitive \(s\) and mutant \(m\) (resistant), with \(s+m=n\). Mutations occur at a rate \(\mu_D\) per division:

\[ \frac{ds}{dt} = r\,(1-\mu_D)\,s , \qquad \frac{dm}{dt} = r\,m + r\,\mu_D\,s . \]

Solving the first, \(s(t) = e^{r(1-\mu_D)t}\), and using \(m = n - s\):

\[ m(t) = e^{rt}\left( 1 - e^{-r\mu_D t}\right) = n(t)\left( 1 - e^{-r\mu_D t}\right) , \]

\[ \boxed{\ \langle M\rangle = N\left(1-e^{-r\mu_D T}\right) \simeq N\,\mu_D\, rT = N\,\mu_D \log N\ } \]

using \(rT=\log N\) and assuming \(\mu_D \ll 1\). Note the extra \(\log N\) relative to hypothesis A: mutants that arose early have been multiplying ever since.

Inverting it,

\[ \widehat\mu_D = -\frac{1}{rT}\log\left(1-\frac MN\right) \simeq \frac{1}{\log N}\frac MN , \]

Note that the distribution we are about to derive is so broad that this is not a good estimator (see the appendix, Rosche & Foster 2000).

The exact recursion

Count births rather than time (no death). When the population is \(n\) with \(m\) mutants the next newborn is a mutant with probability \(p_m(m,n)\) and sensitive with probability \(p_s(m,n)=1-p_m(m,n)\), so that

\[ P(m+1\mid n+1) = p_m(m,n)\,P(m\mid n) + p_s(m+1,n)\,P(m+1\mid n) . \]

Both probabilities follow from the birth rates: sensitive cells are born at rate \(r_s = r\,(1-\mu_D)\,s\) and mutants at rate \(r_m = r\,m + r\,\mu_D\,s\), with \(s = n-m\), so the total birth rate is \(r_s + r_m = r\,(n-m) + r\,m = r\,n\) and

\[ p_m(m,n) = \frac{r_m}{r_s+r_m} = \frac{m + \mu_D\,(n-m)}{n} = \mu_D + (1-\mu_D)\,\frac{m}{n} , \qquad p_s(m,n) = 1 - p_m(m,n) = (1-\mu_D)\left(1-\frac{m}{n}\right) . \]

This recursion is exact. It can also be solved exactly, with a generating function as in L2: see the appendix (Lea & Coulson 1949; Gunnarsson, Leder & Foo 2021).

Heuristics and approximation of the tail

When did the first mutant appear? Set \(m=0\) in the recursion:

\[ P(0\mid n+1) = (1-\mu_D)\,P(0\mid n) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(0\mid n) = (1-\mu_D)^n \simeq e^{-\mu_D n} . \]

So the population size \(n_\mu\) at which the first mutation occurs has density

\[ \rho(n_\mu) = P(0\mid n_\mu) - P(0\mid n_\mu+1) \simeq \mu_D\, e^{-\mu_D n_\mu} . \]

How big is its clone at the end? If mutations are rare we can neglect the source term, \(\dot m \approx rm\), so

\[ \frac{dm}{dn} = \frac{\dot m}{\dot n} \approx \frac mn \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m = \frac{n}{n_\mu} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{N}{n_\mu} . \]

A mutation that happens when the culture is small produces a jackpot; one that happens at the last division contributes a single cell.

Change variables \(n_\mu = N/M\), \(|dn_\mu/dM| = N/M^2\):

\[ \boxed{\ P(M\mid N) \simeq \frac{N\mu_D}{M^{2}}\;e^{-\mu_D N/M} \ \simeq\ \frac{N\mu_D}{M^{2}}\quad (M \gg \mu_D N)\ } \]

or in terms of the frequency \(X=M/N\), \(P(X)\simeq \mu_D X^{-2}e^{-\mu_D/X}\). A power-law tail with exponent 2, cut off at \(M\sim N\) because a clone cannot be bigger than the culture, and suppressed below \(M\sim \mu_D N\) because the first mutation cannot be arbitrarily late.

Moments

The tail controls everything. With the cut-offs \(1\le M\le N\):

\[ \langle M\rangle = \int_1^N \! dM\; M\,P(M\mid N) \simeq N\mu_D \log N , \qquad \langle M^2\rangle = \int_1^N \! dM\; M^2\,P(M\mid N) \simeq N^{2}\mu_D , \]

so that the squared coefficient of variation is

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{\operatorname{var}M}{\langle M\rangle^{2}} = \frac{\langle M^2\rangle}{\langle M\rangle^2}-1 \simeq \frac{1}{\mu_D \log^2 N} - 1 \ } \]

Compare the two hypotheses at equal mean:

acquired spontaneous
distribution Poisson \(\sim N\mu/M^2\)
\(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle\) \(1\) \(\sim N/\log N\)
\(\mathrm{CV}\) \(1/\sqrt{\langle M\rangle}\) \(\sqrt{N/(\langle M\rangle \log N)}\)

The spontaneous hypothesis has a CV larger by a factor \(\sqrt{N}\) — eight orders of magnitude in \(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle\) for \(N=10^8\). This is what Luria and Delbrück measured to establish that mutations are spontaneous.

In HiDenSeq (Husain et al. 2026, canavanine resistance in budding yeast) the replicate cultures are DNA-barcoded and grown in one pot, and colony counting is replaced by deep sequencing of the barcodes, which pushes the number of replicates above \(10^5\). The \(1/M^2\) tail is then visible directly.

Where the heuristic breaks. Keeping only the descendants of the first mutant, the same integral gives \(\langle M\rangle \simeq N\mu_D\log(1/\mu_D)\), not \(N\mu_D\log N\). The two agree only when \(\mu_D N = O(1)\), i.e. when there really is about one mutational event per culture. For \(\mu_D N \gg 1\) the later mutations matter and the exact treatment is needed — the tail exponent, however, is unchanged.

Appendix. Solving the recursion

Not covered in class. The recursion of the main text can be solved with the tool of L2: a generating function turns it into a partial differential equation, and the method of characteristics solves that.

Define \(G_n(z)=\sum_m P(m\mid n)\,z^m\). Multiply the recursion by \(z^m\), sum over \(m\), and use \(p_m(m,n)=\mu_D+(1-\mu_D)\,m/n\) together with \(\sum_m m\,z^m P(m\mid n) = z\,\partial_z G_n\):

\[ G_{n+1}(z) = \bigl[\mu_D\,z + (1-\mu_D)\bigr]\,G_n(z) + (1-\mu_D)\,\frac{z(z-1)}{n}\,\frac{\partial G_n}{\partial z} . \]

Both terms are exact. For \(n\gg1\) we can treat \(n\) as continuous, and to first order in \(\mu_D\),

\[ \frac{\partial G}{\partial n} = \mu_D\,(z-1)\,G + \frac{z(z-1)}{n}\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial z} , \qquad G(z,n=1) = 1 , \]

the initial condition saying that the culture starts from one sensitive cell. Along the characteristics

\[ \frac{dz}{dn} = -\frac{z(z-1)}{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C \equiv n\,\frac{z-1}{z} = \text{constant} , \qquad z-1 = \frac{C}{n-C} , \]

the equation becomes an ordinary one, \(d\log G/dn = \mu_D\,(z-1) = \mu_D\,C/(n-C)\), so \(\log G = \mu_D\,C\log\frac{n-C}{1-C}\). Putting \(C\) back,

\[ \boxed{\ \log G(z,n) = \mu_D\,n\,\frac{z-1}{z}\,\log\frac{n}{\,n-z(n-1)\,}\ } \]

Three things follow at once:

  • \(z\to0\) gives \(\log G \to -\mu_D (n-1)\), that is \(P(M=0)=e^{-\mu_D N}\): the \(p_0\) used in the main text.
  • Expanding around \(z=1\) gives \(\langle M\rangle = \partial_z G|_{z=1} = \mu_D\,N\log N\), the mean obtained from the deterministic calculation.
  • For \(N\gg1\) at fixed \(z<1\), \(n-z(n-1)\simeq n(1-z)\) and

\[ G(z) = \exp\left[\,m_{\text{ev}}\,\frac{1-z}{z}\,\log(1-z)\right] , \qquad m_{\text{ev}} = \mu_D N , \]

the Lea–Coulson generating function. Its logarithmic singularity at \(z\to1\) is the \(1/M^2\) tail, and what keeps \(\langle M\rangle\) finite is precisely the \(n-z(n-1)\) we just dropped — the culture is finite.

The same result, read probabilistically. Mutational events are a Poisson process along divisions, with mean \(m_{\text{ev}}=\mu_D N\); an event at population size \(n\) founds a clone that ends with \(j=N/n\) cells, and \(n\) uniform on \([1,N]\) gives \(P(j)=1/j^2\) (exactly, \(P(j)=1/[j(j+1)]\)). The total number of mutants is then a sum of a Poisson number of clones — a compound Poisson — whose generating function is \(G(z)=\exp\{m_{\text{ev}}[\phi(z)-1]\}\) with

\[ \phi(z) = \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{z^{j}}{j(j+1)} = 1 + \frac{1-z}{z}\log(1-z) , \]

the same \(G\). Jackpots are clones founded early, and the \(1/j^2\) clone sizes are what make the tail heavy.

Partial plating. If a fraction \(\varepsilon\) of the culture is plated, each mutant is counted independently with probability \(\varepsilon\), so the count has generating function \(G(1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon z)\). In particular the probability of an empty plate is

\[ p_0(\varepsilon) = G(1-\varepsilon) = \exp\left[-\,m_{\text{ev}}\,\frac{\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon)}{1-\varepsilon}\right] , \]

which reduces to \(e^{-m_{\text{ev}}}\) as \(\varepsilon\to1\) and is the form to invert when estimating \(\mu_D\) from empty plates.

Appendix. Measuring the mutation rate from jackpots

Not covered in class. Since \(\langle M\rangle\) is dominated by rare plates, the sample mean is a terrible estimator: it has enormous variance and is strongly biased downward in small experiments. Three better options, in increasing order of efficiency:

1. The \(p_0\) method. Count only the fraction of cultures with zero resistant colonies. That is a Poisson probability, and nothing else enters:

\[ p_0 = e^{-\mu_D N} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ \widehat\mu_D = -\frac{\log p_0}{N}\ } \]

Robust, immune to the tail, and useless if \(p_0\) is near 0 or 1 — so the experiment must be designed with \(\mu_D N \sim 1\). When only a fraction \(\varepsilon\) of each culture is plated, use the corrected \(p_0\) of the appendix.

2. Maximum likelihood. Invert \(G(z)\) numerically for \(P(M\mid N)\) and maximise the likelihood over \(\mu_D\). The practical recipe is the discrete convolution of Ma, Sandri & Sarkar 1992, used by all modern fluctuation-test software.

3. Median-based estimators. The median of the distribution is well behaved even when the mean is not (Lea & Coulson’s method).

The general lesson. When a distribution has a heavy tail, estimate its parameters from the bulk or from the probability of zero, not from the mean.

Notebook

  1. Simulate the fluctuation test: stochastic exponential growth from one cell with mutation at rate \(\mu_D\) per division, scored at \(N\). Repeat \(10^3\) times.
  2. Plot the survival function \(P(M>x)\) on log–log axes and read off the exponent \(-1\) (i.e. \(P(M)\sim M^{-2}\)).
  3. Simulate the Lamarckian null (binomial at the end) and compare the two histograms at the same mean. Compute \(\operatorname{var}M/\langle M\rangle\) for both.
  4. Estimate \(\mu_D\) with the \(p_0\) method and by maximum likelihood; compare their bias and spread as a function of the number of cultures.
  5. Data. Luria & Delbrück’s original tables (1943): apply the estimators and compare with the published numbers. Then the HiDenSeq distributions (Husain et al. 2026), deep enough to fit the tail exponent instead of assuming it.

Exercises

  1. Show that the Poisson limit of the binomial requires only \(N\to\infty\), \(\mu_L\to0\) with \(\mu_L N\) fixed, and estimate the error for \(N=10^8\), \(\mu_L=10^{-8}\).
  2. Derive \(\phi(z)=1+\frac{1-z}{z}\log(1-z)\) from \(P(j)=1/[j(j+1)]\), and verify \(G(0)=e^{-m_{\text{ev}}}\).
  3. Work out \(\langle M\rangle\) and \(\langle M^2\rangle\) with explicit cut-offs and show which cut-off dominates each.
  4. Suppose mutants grow with rate \(r'\ne r\). Show that the clone-size exponent becomes \(1+r/r'\), so that the tail is a measurement of the mutant’s fitness cost.
  5. Design a fluctuation test: given \(\mu_D\approx10^{-8}\) and \(N\approx10^{8}\), how many cultures are needed to estimate \(\mu_D\) within 10% by the \(p_0\) method?
  6. Why does the Lamarckian hypothesis predict a Fano factor of exactly 1, while any history-dependent mechanism predicts more? (This is the general logic of a fluctuation test.)
