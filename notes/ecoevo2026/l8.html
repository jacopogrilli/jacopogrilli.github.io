Lecture 8. What is evolution?

In L7 we wrote how the frequency of a type changes under selection. Here, a step back: what evolution is, what changes, by which mechanisms, and how we know it happened.

Two quotes

  • “If you ask me about my innermost conviction whether our century will be called the century of iron or the century of steam or electricity, I answer without hesitation: it will be called the century of the mechanical view of nature, the century of Darwin.” L. Boltzmann, 1886.
  • “Nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution.” T. Dobzhansky (1973).

  • S. J. Gould, The Panda’s Thumb (1980), and all the others by Gould
  • R. Dawkins, The Selfish Gene (1976).

Species are not fundamental

  • We classify living things into species. The usual criterion: two individuals belong to the same species if they can interbreed and have fertile offspring.
  • Ring species break the criterion. Neighbouring populations interbreed all around a ring, but the two ends of the ring, where they meet, do not. Example: the greenish warbler around the Tibetan plateau (Irwin, Bensch & Price 2001).
  • A threshold on genetic similarity does not work either: there is no natural cutoff.
  • Bacteria reproduce asexually and exchange genes: the interbreeding criterion does not even apply.
  • The species is a useful classification, not the result of a fundamental process. We choose the units (species, strains, types) according to the question. In L6 and L7 the same equations described two species and two types of the same species.

Evolution is change in heritable characteristics

Evolution is the change, over generations, of the heritable characteristics of a population.

  • Heritable: passed from parent(s) to offspring.
    • The genotype (the genetic material) is inherited. The phenotype (the traits: beak depth, resistance to a drug, cell shape) is the expression of the genotype in an environment.
    • Culture is also passed on, without genes.
  • Variation: individuals differ. Sources of heritable variation:
    • mutations, errors in copying the DNA (L3);
    • recombination, the mixing of the parents’ DNA in sexual reproduction;
    • gene flow between individuals. In bacteria, genes move between cells, for instance on plasmids (small DNA molecules separate from the chromosome), and are then inherited.
  • Change: what changes is the frequency of traits in a population, not the individuals. This is the \(x_i\) of L7.

Mechanisms

What changes the frequencies:

  • Natural selection. Heritable differences in survival and reproduction. The expected number of offspring is the fitness (L7).
    • Selection acts on variation that already exists. It does not create it (L3).
    • It can be tracked at different levels: genes, individuals, groups.
  • Sexual selection. In sexual species, what matters is also finding a partner. Traits that attract partners (bright colours, large ornaments) can spread even if they cost survival, for instance by making the bearer easier to spot for predators.
  • Genetic drift. Chance: which individuals reproduce is random, as in L2. It changes frequencies even with no fitness differences.
  • Migration (gene flow between populations). It brings in variation from elsewhere.

What evolution is not

  • Not a path towards something better, or towards more complexity.
    • Gould’s “left wall”: there is a minimal complexity for a living thing, and variation can only spread away from it. The most complex organisms get more complex over time, but the typical organism was and is a bacterium (Gould, Full House).
  • Evolution also simplifies.
    • Parasites lose genes and organs they no longer need.
    • Multicellularity has been lost, too.
    • Cave animals lose their eyes.
  • Complexity has costs. The human brain is about 2% of the body mass and uses about 20% of the energy at rest. Where resources are scarce, a smaller brain can be better.
  • Not an optimizer of design. Evolution modifies what is there (next section, poor design). What selection maximizes, if anything, is a question for L14.

Outcomes

  • Adaptation: the population becomes better suited to its environment (beak depth after a drought, resistance to a drug, L7).
  • Speciation: one lineage splits into two that no longer interbreed.
  • Extinction.

The time scale sets the vocabulary:

  • Microevolution: change of frequencies within a population, over generations we can observe.
  • Macroevolution: origin of new species and groups, over geological times. Example: the closest living relatives of whales are the hippopotamuses. The two share a common ancestor, and whales descend from land artiodactyls (Thewissen et al. 2007).
  • The boundary between the two is arbitrary.

Evidence for macroevolution

  • Exaptation: a structure evolved for one function is co-opted for another (Gould & Vrba 1982). Feathers appeared in dinosaurs, probably for insulation or display, and were later used to fly.
  • Convergence: unrelated lineages find similar solutions. Flight in insects, birds and bats. Camera eyes in vertebrates and cephalopods.
  • Poor design, explained by history:
    • the vertebrate retina has the photoreceptors behind the nerve fibres, which leave the eye through a blind spot; in cephalopods the order is the opposite, with no blind spot;
    • human childbirth: a large head through a pelvis shaped by walking upright;
    • RuBisCO, the enzyme that fixes CO\(_2\) in photosynthesis, is slow and also reacts with O\(_2\). It evolved when the atmosphere had almost no oxygen.
  • Vestigial structures and atavisms:
    • vestigial: reduced structures without their original function (the human coccyx, the eyes of cave fish);
    • atavism: an ancestral trait that reappears in a few individuals (whales with hind limbs, humans born with a tail). The instructions are still in the genome.
  • Comparative anatomy: the same bones in the forelimb of a human, a bat and a whale.
  • Predictions from the fossil record. Tiktaalik, a fish with features of land vertebrates (neck, robust fins with wrist-like bones), was found where it was predicted: in freshwater rocks about 375 million years old, on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic (Daeschler, Shubin & Jenkins 2006; N. Shubin, Your Inner Fish).

Evolution we can watch

  • Drosophila on different food. Populations of D. pseudoobscura kept for about a year on starch or on maltose medium then preferred to mate with flies from their own medium: the beginning of reproductive isolation (Dodd 1989).
  • Bacteria and antibiotics:
    • resistant mutants exist before the drug (Luria & Delbrück 1943, L3);
    • resistance evolves across a gradient of drug on a giant plate in about 11 days (Baym et al. 2016, L3);
    • resistance is selected at concentrations far below the MIC (L7).
  • Lenski’s long-term evolution experiment (LTEE). Twelve populations of E. coli in the same simple medium since 1988, transferred every day.
    • Fitness keeps increasing (Lenski et al. 1991; Wiser, Ribeck & Lenski 2013), L10.
    • In one population two lineages coexist, each favoured when rare (Rozen & Lenski 2000, L7).
    • In another, after about 31,000 generations, the bacteria evolved the ability to grow on citrate, present in the medium all along (Blount, Borland & Lenski 2008).
  • Darwin’s finches. Beak depth changed within one year of drought, and changed back and forth over decades (Grant & Grant 2002, L7).
  • Guppies. Moved from streams with predators to streams without, their life history (age and size at maturity) evolved within a few years (Reznick et al. 1997).
