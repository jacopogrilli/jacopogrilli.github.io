# Lecture 6. Competition

---

In [L4](l4.html) and [L5](l5.html) a population was limited by its resources and by itself. What happens if you put two species in the same tube? Who wins? Can we predict it from how each species grows alone?

## Two species in a tube

Gause did the experiment in the early 1930s, with yeasts and with *Paramecium*, measuring each species alone and in mixture ([Gause 1932](https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.9.4.389); [Gause 1934](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.79.2036.16-a); data in [gauseR](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.6926)).

So the same experiment gives exclusion with one pair and coexistence with another. We want a model where the outcome follows from parameters that can be measured, in part, on each species alone.

## Lotka–Volterra competition

Each species alone grows logistically, as in [L4](l4.html). Together in the tube, each is also slowed down by the other:

$$
\boxed{\ \frac{dN_1}{dt} = r_1N_1\left(1-\frac{N_1+\alpha_{12}N_2}{K_1}\right),\qquad
\frac{dN_2}{dt} = r_2N_2\left(1-\frac{N_2+\alpha_{21}N_1}{K_2}\right)\ }
$$

This is the Lotka–Volterra model of competition (Lotka 1925; [Volterra 1926](https://doi.org/10.1038/118558a0)).

- $r_i$ is the growth rate of species $i$ alone at low density, as in L1, and $K_i$ its carrying capacity alone (L4's $N_{\max}$).
- $\alpha_{ij}\ge0$ is the *competition coefficient*: one individual of $j$ slows the growth of $i$ as much as $\alpha_{ij}$ individuals of $i$ do. $\alpha_{ij}=1$: for $i$, a competitor is the same as a conspecific.
- The signs of $\alpha_{12}$ and $\alpha_{21}$ set the type of interaction. With $\alpha_{ij}<0$, $j$ helps $i$. Both negative is mutualism, opposite signs is exploitation (one eats or parasitizes the other, L12). Today both are positive.

Measuring each density in units of its own carrying capacity, $u_i=N_i/K_i$,

$$
\frac{du_1}{dt} = r_1u_1\,(1-u_1-a_{12}u_2),\qquad
\frac{du_2}{dt} = r_2u_2\,(1-u_2-a_{21}u_1),\qquad
a_{12}=\alpha_{12}\frac{K_2}{K_1},\quad a_{21}=\alpha_{21}\frac{K_1}{K_2}.
$$

$a_{ij}$ is the competition from $j$ *at its own carrying capacity*, measured against $i$'s. The carrying capacities only set the units of the densities, and $r_1$ the unit of time. Three parameters are left: $a_{12}$, $a_{21}$ and $r_2/r_1$. We will see that the possible outcomes depend only on $a_{12}$ and $a_{21}$. The ratio $r_2/r_1$ changes the trajectories, and so, when two outcomes are possible, which one is reached from a given start.

## Fixed points

Setting both equations to zero, each species is either absent, $u_i=0$, or has zero per-capita growth, $u_i+a_{ij}u_j=1$. There are $2^2=4$ fixed points (with $S$ species, up to $2^S$):

| | $u_1^*$ | $u_2^*$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| extinction | $0$ | $0$ |
| 1 alone | $1$ | $0$ |
| 2 alone | $0$ | $1$ |
| coexistence | $\dfrac{1-a_{12}}{1-a_{12}a_{21}}$ | $\dfrac{1-a_{21}}{1-a_{12}a_{21}}$ |

The coexistence point solves the linear system $u_1+a_{12}u_2=1$, $a_{21}u_1+u_2=1$. It is a population only if both $u_i^*$ are positive. In the original units, $N_i^*=K_iu_i^*$.

### Nullclines

The graphical method of [L4](l4.html#graphical-method-for-long-term-behavior) in two dimensions. In the $(u_1,u_2)$ plane:

- $\dot u_1=0$ on the axis $u_1=0$ and on the line $u_1+a_{12}u_2=1$, from $(1,0)$ to $(0,1/a_{12})$. Below the line $u_1$ grows, above it it declines.
- $\dot u_2=0$ on the axis $u_2=0$ and on the line $a_{21}u_1+u_2=1$, from $(1/a_{21},0)$ to $(0,1)$. Below the line $u_2$ grows.

Fixed points are where a nullcline of $u_1$ meets one of $u_2$. The two lines can sit in four ways (one above the other, either way, or crossing, either way), and these are the four outcomes below. Which one is set by comparing the intercepts on each axis: $1$ with $1/a_{21}$, and $1/a_{12}$ with $1$.

## Linear stability in two dimensions

The same as in [L4](l4.html#fixed-points-and-their-stability), now with a vector. Write $\dot u_i = f_i(u)$ and $u=u^*+\delta$, with $\delta$ small. Expanding, with $f(u^*)=0$,

$$
\frac{d\delta_i}{dt} \simeq \sum_j J_{ij}\,\delta_j,\qquad J_{ij}=\left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial u_j}\right|_{u^*}.
$$
$J$ is the *Jacobian*. Close to $u^*$ the dynamics is the linear system $\dot\delta=J\delta$, solved in [Appendix S1](s1.html). We need two results from it. The fixed point is stable if every eigenvalue of $J$ has a negative real part, and unstable if at least one has a positive real part ([S1](s1.html#long-times-and-stability)). In two dimensions the trace and the determinant decide ([S1](s1.html#two-dimensions)):

$$
\text{stable}\iff \operatorname{tr}J<0\ \text{ and }\ \det J>0 .
$$

If $\det J<0$ the eigenvalues are real with opposite signs: a *saddle*, attracting along one direction and repelling along the other.

Here $f_i=u_ig_i$, with $g_i=r_i(1-u_i-a_{ij}u_j)$ the per-capita growth rate, so

$$
J_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\,g_i(u^*) + u_i^*\,\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial u_j}.
$$

Two kinds of rows:

- if species $i$ is **absent**, $u_i^*=0$, its row is zero except $J_{ii}=g_i(u^*)$: *the per-capita growth rate of $i$ when rare*;
- if species $i$ is **present**, $g_i(u^*)=0$ and $J_{ij}=u_i^*\,\partial g_i/\partial u_j$.

### Extinction

$J=\operatorname{diag}(r_1,r_2)$: unstable. Either species alone grows.

### One species alone

At $(1,0)$,

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix} -r_1 & -r_1a_{12}\\ 0 & r_2\,(1-a_{21})\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$J$ is triangular, so its eigenvalues are on the diagonal:

- $-r_1$: species 1 returns to its carrying capacity at rate $r_1$, as alone in L4;
- $\lambda_{2|1}=r_2\,(1-a_{21})$: the per-capita growth rate of species 2, rare, in the environment set by species 1 at its carrying capacity. This is the *invasion growth rate* of 2 into 1.

So 1 alone is stable if and only if 2 cannot invade it, $a_{21}>1$. In the same way, at $(0,1)$, $\lambda_{1|2}=r_1(1-a_{12})$, and 2 alone is stable if and only if $a_{12}>1$.

**Invasion analysis.** This is a method in itself, and the most useful one when the Jacobian is out of reach. Let one species, the *resident*, sit alone at its carrying capacity, and add a few individuals of the other, the *invader*. While it is rare, the invader does not change the resident, and it grows or declines exponentially at its invasion growth rate. Then: coexistence if each species can invade the other, exclusion if only one can, bistability if neither can ([The four outcomes](#the-four-outcomes)). Two caveats:

- invading is not replacing: an invader that grows when rare can end up coexisting with the resident, or excluding it;
- the test probes one direction only. A positive invasion rate makes the resident's fixed point unstable, but a negative one does not make it stable, since it could be unstable in another direction. Here the test is exact, because $J$ is triangular and the other eigenvalue, $-r_i$, is always negative.

### Coexistence

$$
J=-\begin{pmatrix} r_1u_1^* & r_1a_{12}u_1^*\\ r_2a_{21}u_2^* & r_2u_2^*\end{pmatrix},
$$

$$
\qquad
\operatorname{tr}J=-r_1u_1^*-r_2u_2^*<0,
\qquad
\det J=r_1r_2\,u_1^*u_2^*\,(1-a_{12}a_{21}).
$$

When it is feasible, the trace is always negative. So coexistence is stable if $a_{12}a_{21}<1$ and a saddle if $a_{12}a_{21}>1$.

## The four outcomes

Putting the three results together, the outcome depends only on $a_{12}=\alpha_{12}K_2/K_1$ and $a_{21}=\alpha_{21}K_1/K_2$, i.e. on how $\alpha_{12}$ compares with $K_1/K_2$ and $\alpha_{21}$ with $K_2/K_1$:

| | condition | feasible fixed points | stable | outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $a_{12}<1$, $a_{21}<1$ | 4 | coexistence | **coexistence** |
| 2 | $a_{12}<1$, $a_{21}>1$ | 3 | $(1,0)$ | **competitive exclusion**: 1 wins |
| 3 | $a_{12}>1$, $a_{21}<1$ | 3 | $(0,1)$ | **competitive exclusion**: 2 wins |
| 4 | $a_{12}>1$, $a_{21}>1$ | 4 | $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ | **bistability** |

- **Coexistence.** Each species limits itself more than it limits the other. The two may share resources, but individuals of the same species affect each other more. From any start with both species present, the populations go to the coexistence point.
- **Competitive exclusion.** One species is the stronger competitor: it can invade the other, and the other cannot invade it. There is no coexistence point with both densities positive.
- **Bistability.** Each species, once established, keeps the other out. The coexistence point exists but is a saddle. Its stable direction, the *separatrix*, divides the starts that end with 1 alone from those that end with 2 alone. Which species wins depends on where the populations start. The shape of the separatrix depends on $r_2/r_1$. If $r_1=r_2=r$ it is a straight line through the origin and the saddle, $u_2/u_1=(a_{21}-1)/(a_{12}-1)$: on that line $\frac{d}{dt}\ln(u_2/u_1)=r\,[(1-a_{21})u_1-(1-a_{12})u_2]=0$, so a trajectory that starts on it stays on it. It is the diagonal only if also $a_{12}=a_{21}$. For $r_1\ne r_2$ it is curved, and found numerically (figure in [Nullclines](#nullclines)).

In all four cases extinction is unstable: from a few individuals of each, both populations first grow. Where they end up depends on $a_{12}$ and $a_{21}$. The growth rates $r_1$, $r_2$ set how fast, and in the bistable case where the separatrix lies, but not which outcomes are possible.

## Adding mortality

Now add a mortality $\delta$, the same for both species (a dilution removes cells regardless of species), as at the end of [L4](l4.html#extinction):

$$
\frac{dN_i}{dt} = r_iN_i\left(1-\frac{N_i+\alpha_{ij}N_j}{K_i}\right)-\delta N_i
= (r_i-\delta)\,N_i\left(1-\frac{N_i+\alpha_{ij}N_j}{\tilde K_i}\right),
\qquad
\tilde K_i = K_i\left(1-\frac{\delta}{r_i}\right).
$$

It is the same model, with $r_i\to r_i-\delta$, $K_i\to\tilde K_i$, and the same $\alpha_{ij}$. The algebra is the same as in L4: mortality lowers each carrying capacity linearly, to zero at $\delta=r_i$. Everything above holds with $\tilde K$, and in particular

$$
\boxed{\ a_{ij}(\delta)=\alpha_{ij}\,\frac{\tilde K_j}{\tilde K_i} = a_{ij}\,\frac{1-\delta/r_j}{1-\delta/r_i}\ }
$$

**Why the fast grower is favoured.** Mortality removes a fraction $\delta/r_i$ of each carrying capacity. The slow grower loses the larger fraction. Call $f$ the faster species and $s$ the slower one, $r_f>r_s$. Taking logs,

$$
\ln a_{fs}(\delta)=\ln a_{fs}-\Phi(\delta),\qquad
\ln a_{sf}(\delta)=\ln a_{sf}+\Phi(\delta),\qquad
\Phi(\delta)=\ln\frac{1-\delta/r_f}{1-\delta/r_s}.
$$

$\Phi(0)=0$, and $\Phi$ increases with $\delta$ and diverges at $\delta\to r_s$. Mortality only shifts the pair $(\ln a_{fs},\ln a_{sf})$, by $\Phi$ along the direction $(-1,+1)$. Three consequences:

1. **The product $a_{fs}a_{sf}$ does not depend on $\delta$.** The pair moves along a line at $-45°$ in the space $(\ln a_{fs},\ln a_{sf})$. Whether the transition passes through coexistence or through bistability is fixed by the pair at $\delta=0$: coexistence if $a_{fs}a_{sf}<1$, bistability if $a_{fs}a_{sf}>1$.
2. **Mortality can only help the fast grower.** A reversal needs a slow grower that wins at low mortality ($a_{fs}>1$), i.e. a *tradeoff* between growth rate and competitive ability. Without it, the fast grower wins at every $\delta$.
3. **The slow grower is excluded before mortality alone would kill it.** As $\delta\to r_s$, $\Phi\to\infty$. Whatever the $a_{ij}$, the fast grower wins before $\delta$ reaches $r_s$. Competition drives a species extinct at a mortality it could survive alone.

The two transitions are at $\Phi(\delta)=\ln a_{fs}$ (the fast grower starts to invade) and $\Phi(\delta)=-\ln a_{sf}$ (the slow grower stops invading). Solving $\Phi(\delta)=c$,

$$
\delta=\frac{e^{c}-1}{e^{c}/r_s-1/r_f}.
$$

In the coexistence window the density of the fast grower is $u_f^*=\frac{1-a_{fs}(\delta)}{1-a_{fs}a_{sf}}$ (in units of $\tilde K_f$). The denominator is constant, so its share grows with $\delta$.

## Serial dilution is mortality, again

In [L5](l5.html) a daily dilution by a factor $D$ was treated as a mortality $\delta=\ln D/T$ per unit time ($T$ = one cycle). That was an approximation: the Allee per-capita rate is not linear in the density. For Lotka–Volterra it is exact, as far as invasion is concerned (Abreu et al., Supplementary Note 2, state the same).

Within a cycle there is no death, only growth. A resident $s$, alone and at its periodic steady state, must grow by exactly a factor $D$ in each cycle:

$$
\ln D=\int_0^T r_s\left(1-\frac{N_s(t)}{K_s}\right)dt
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\bar N_s\equiv\frac1T\int_0^TN_s\,dt = K_s\left(1-\frac{\delta}{r_s}\right)=\tilde K_s .
$$

A rare invader $f$ grows during the cycle and is diluted at its end:

$$
\ln\frac{N_f(T)}{D\,N_f(0)}=\int_0^T r_f\left(1-\alpha_{fs}\frac{N_s(t)}{K_f}\right)dt-\ln D
=T\,(r_f-\delta)\left(1-\alpha_{fs}\frac{\tilde K_s}{\tilde K_f}\right)=T\,\lambda_{f|s}(\delta).
$$

This is exactly the continuous-time invasion rate. The per-capita rate is linear in the densities, so only the *time average* of the resident matters, and the resident's own balance fixes that average. The coexistence densities are not the same as in continuous time, and nothing here holds once the per-capita rate is nonlinear (the Allee effect of L5) or with lag phases.

## Many species with symmetric interactions

With $S$ species, the natural generalization keeps each per-capita growth rate linear in all the densities, $\dot N_i = N_i\,g_i$ with $g_i = r_i+\sum_j A_{ij}N_j$. This is the *generalized Lotka–Volterra* (GLV) model. $A_{ii}<0$ is self-limitation, and the sign of $A_{ij}$ is the sign of the effect of $j$ on $i$. (In class: $g_i=r_i-\sum_j\beta_{ij}N_j$, so $\beta=-A$, positive for competition.)

The fixed points are found as for two species. Each species is either absent, $N_i^*=0$, or present, with $g_i=0$. Once we choose which species are present, $g_i=0$ for them is a linear system, $\sum_jA_{ij}N_j^*=-r_i$ with $i$ and $j$ running over the present species, which in general has one solution. Each species can be present or absent, so there are $2^S$ candidates. Each has to be checked: is it feasible (all present densities positive)? Is it stable? The work grows exponentially with $S$, and more than one fixed point can be stable (multistability, the $S$-species version of bistability).

There is a special case where none of this checking is needed. When $A$ is symmetric there is a function that always decreases along trajectories:

$$
L(N) = -\sum_i r_iN_i - \frac12\sum_{ij}A_{ij}N_iN_j,
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial N_i}=-g_i,
\qquad
\frac{dL}{dt}=\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial N_i}\,\dot N_i = -\sum_i N_i\,g_i^2\ \le 0 .
$$

It stops decreasing only at fixed points (at all of them, unstable ones included). $L$ is not a potential: $\dot N_i=-N_i\,\partial L/\partial N_i$, and because of the factor $N_i$ the flow is not the gradient of $L$. It is a *Lyapunov function*: it only has to decrease along trajectories, and that is enough. The dynamics goes downhill on $L$, as on the 1D potential of [L5](l5.html#normal-form-of-the-cusp-and-the-potential).

If, in addition, $-A$ is positive definite (self-limitation dominates), $L$ is a convex bowl and has a single minimum on the region $N_i\ge0$. Every trajectory ends there, so there is no bistability and there are no cycles. This is the minimum of $L$ on $N_i\ge0$, not in general the point where all $\partial L/\partial N_i=0$: that point solves $\sum_jA_{ij}N_j=-r_i$ for all $i$, and some of its densities can be negative. At the minimum, each species is either

- present, with $\partial L/\partial N_i=0$, i.e. $g_i=0$; or
- absent, with $\partial L/\partial N_i\ge0$, i.e. $g_i\le0$: it cannot invade.

The state reached is the one that no absent species can invade: the $S$-species version of the invasion criterion. This is MacArthur's "minimum principle" ([MacArthur 1970](https://doi.org/10.1016/0040-5809(70)90039-0)), and L17–L18 build on it.

**Two species.** With $A_{ij}=-r_i\alpha_{ij}/K_i$, $A$ itself is not symmetric. But the argument only needs $w_iA_{ij}$ symmetric for some weights $w_i>0$. Then $L=-\sum_iw_ir_iN_i-\frac12\sum_{ij}w_iA_{ij}N_iN_j$ gives $dL/dt=-\sum_iw_iN_ig_i^2$. With two species such weights always exist when $A_{12}$ and $A_{21}$ have the same sign: take $w_1/w_2=A_{21}/A_{12}$. So any two competitors have a Lyapunov function, whatever $\alpha_{12}$ and $\alpha_{21}$. The quadratic part of $L$ is positive definite if and only if $A_{11}A_{22}>A_{12}A_{21}$, that is $\alpha_{12}\alpha_{21}=a_{12}a_{21}<1$: one outcome, coexistence or exclusion. If $a_{12}a_{21}>1$, $L$ is not convex: two minima on $N_i\ge0$ if both $a_{ij}>1$ (bistability), still one if only one of them is (exclusion). The product $a_{12}a_{21}$ is the one that mortality leaves unchanged ([Adding mortality](#adding-mortality)).

From three species on, the weights must satisfy $w_iA_{ij}=w_jA_{ji}$ for every pair at once. They exist only if $A_{12}A_{23}A_{31}=A_{13}A_{32}A_{21}$, and the same for every loop of species. Generic interactions break this, and then there is in general no such function. (A potential would need more: a symmetric Jacobian everywhere, [S1](s1.html#a-potential), which GLV does not have even when $A$ is symmetric.) With three species, competition alone can then produce cycles of rock–paper–scissors type ([May & Leonard 1975](https://doi.org/10.1137/0129022)).

## Exercises

1. Write the Jacobian of the competitive LV model at its four fixed points. Check that at the single-species points the eigenvalues are $-r_i$ and the invasion rate of the other species.
2. Show that the coexistence point is feasible and stable if and only if both species can invade, and a saddle if and only if neither can.
3. Mutualism: $\dot N_i = r_iN_i\bigl(1-(N_i-\beta_{ij}N_j)/K_i\bigr)$ with $\beta_{ij}>0$. Show that a stable coexistence point exists if $\beta_{12}\beta_{21}<1$, and that otherwise both populations grow without bound.
4. Species-specific mortality $\delta_i$ (an antibiotic that kills fast growers more). Find the fixed points and their stability.
