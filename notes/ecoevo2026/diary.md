# Class diary

What we covered in each class, with links to the corresponding sections of the notes.

**Tue 22/9** — Logistics and exam, then the shape of the whole course: [what we will write, and what it leaves out](l0.html#what-we-will-write-and-what-it-leaves-out). We built exponential growth from [the simplest scenario, birth and death](l1.html#the-simplest-scenario-birth-and-death), saw the same thing in [discrete generations](l1.html#discrete-generations), and put numbers on it — [orders of magnitude](l1.html#orders-of-magnitude) and [what fraction of all humans ever born is alive today](l1.html#what-fraction-of-all-humans-ever-born-is-alive-today). The rest of the class was about why identical cultures do not grow identically: [the observation](l2.html#the-observation), [what a rate is](l2.html#what-a-rate-is), and the fact that [the mean obeys the deterministic equation](l2.html#the-mean-obeys-the-deterministic-equation). We wrote the [master equation](l2.html#master-equation), and of the [probability generating function](l2.html#probability-generating-function) only the idea, not the derivation.

**Thu 24/9** — We went back to [the observation](l2.html#the-observation) of stochasticity in trajectories and asked what the mean misses: [the second moment and the variance](l2.html#the-second-moment-and-the-variance), the [expected number of deaths](l2.html#expected-number-of-deaths) and the [extinction probability](l2.html#extinction-probability). We derived them from the birth–death process and compared them with experiments. We closed with how to simulate the process exactly, [the Gillespie algorithm](l2.html#simulating-exactly-with-gillespie). [What survivors look like](l2.html#what-survivors-look-like) is left to the notes.

**Tue 29/9** — Mutations. From the [biology](l3.html#biology-intro) (bacteria crossing bands of antibiotic, and the two hypotheses: resistance acquired on contact, or already there by chance) to the [setup](l3.html#setup) of Luria and Delbrück's experiment. Under [acquired resistance](l3.html#hypothesis-a.-acquired-resistance-lamarckian) the counts are Poisson. Under [spontaneous mutation](l3.html#hypothesis-b.-spontaneous-mutation) we computed the mean, wrote [the exact recursion](l3.html#the-exact-recursion), and approximated the distribution by following the first mutant: [heuristics and approximation of the tail](l3.html#heuristics-and-approximation-of-the-tail) and its [moments](l3.html#moments). Same mean, very different fluctuations. The end was rushed and it will be recapped next class.

**Mon 5/10** — First a slower recap of Luria and Delbrück: from the [first mutant](l3.html#heuristics-and-approximation-of-the-tail) to the $1/M^2$ tail and its [moments](l3.html#moments), checked against simulations, the 1943 data and a modern version of the experiment with DNA barcodes. Then we started [L4, limits to growth](l4.html): the exponential curves of L1 eventually flatten, [Malthus](l4.html#malthus)' argument, and Verhulst's [logistic growth](l4.html#logistic-growth-as-an-effective-model), solved exactly and made dimensionless. We stopped before the [graphical method](l4.html#graphical-method-for-long-term-behavior), where the next class starts.

**Wed 7/10** — Long-term behaviour without solving the equation: the [graphical method](l4.html#graphical-method-for-long-term-behavior) and [fixed points and their stability](l4.html#fixed-points-and-their-stability), by linearization. Then we made the limit to growth explicit: [growth on limited resources](l4.html#growth-on-limited-resources), introducing yield and a growth rate that depends on the resource concentration. We derived it from the kinetics of transporters and enzymes ([why growth saturates](l4.html#why-growth-saturates)) and compared with Monod's data. We wrote the equations of a population consuming its resource. The analysis is for the next class.

**Fri 9/10** — After a recap of [why growth saturates](l4.html#why-growth-saturates), we included the resource explicitly in the population dynamics. With a fixed stock of resource, the population grows until the resource is used up. We compared this with the *E. coli* curves. With a constant supply of resource the population settles where the supply feeds the losses. In both cases [the carrying capacity](l4.html#what-a-carrying-capacity-is) is set by the environment, not by the species alone. We closed with [extinction](l4.html#extinction) under increasing mortality, and introduced the transcritical bifurcation.

**Mon 12/10** — Some populations grow *faster* as they become more abundant: [positive density dependence](l5.html#positive-density-dependence), or the Allee effect, with examples from mating to enzymes shared between cells. We wrote [the simplest model](l5.html#the-simplest-model), a per-capita growth rate shaped as a parabola, and read its long-term behaviour with the [graphical method](l5.html#graphical-solution): a weak Allee effect only slows early growth, while a strong one creates a threshold below which the population goes extinct. Then [the experiment](l5.html#the-experiment-yeast-on-sucrose): yeast growing on sucrose, transferred every day into fresh medium, where diluting the culture acts as a mortality. We wrote the model with mortality; its analysis, starting from [changing mortality](l5.html#changing-mortality-in-allee-effect), is for the next class.

**Fri 16/10** — After a recap of the yeast experiment and of the model with mortality, we analysed [what happens when we change mortality](l5.html#changing-mortality-in-allee-effect). We found the [fixed points](l5.html#fixed-points), checked [when they exist and when they are positive](l5.html#feasibility-when-do-the-fixed-points-exist), and computed their [stability](l5.html#stability). Then we redid everything with the graphical method and drew the fixed points as a function of $m$to study the  [two bifurcations](l5.html#two-bifurcations) ([catastrophe and hysteresis](l5.html#catastrophe-and-hysteresis)). We went back to [the yeast data](l5.html#the-experiment-yeast-on-sucrose). After the break we added [immigration](l5.html#immigration-two-folds-and-a-cusp). We started calculating [the fold lines](l5.html#the-bifurcation-points-fold-lines) in the $(m,\lambda)$ plane. Drawing them is for the next class.

**Mon 19/10** — We finished the Allee model with immigration. We calculated [the fold lines](l5.html#the-bifurcation-points-fold-lines) in the $(m,\lambda)$ plane. Shifting the variable to the cusp gives [the normal form of the cusp](l5.html#normal-form-of-the-cusp-and-the-potential) showing that it is the same picture as the mean-field Ising model. Then we started [competition](l6.html) with [Gause's experiments](l6.html#two-species-in-a-tube): two species that grow well alone do not coexist when put together. We wrote the [Lotka–Volterra model](l6.html#lotkavolterra-competition) of competition, found its four [fixed points](l6.html#fixed-points), and started to study their stability with the [nullclines](l6.html#nullclines). We will finish the analysis for all the parameters scenario next time.
