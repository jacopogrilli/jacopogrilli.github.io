Lecture 0. Introduction

Why we write models at all

On Exactitude in Science

…In that Empire, the Art of Cartography attained such Perfection that the map of a single Province occupied the entirety of a City, and the map of the Empire, the entirety of a Province. In time, those Unconscionable Maps no longer satisfied, and the Cartographers Guilds struck a Map of the Empire whose size was that of the Empire, and which coincided point for point with it. The following Generations, who were not so fond of the Study of Cartography as their Forebears had been, saw that that vast Map was Useless, and not without some Pitilessness was it, that they delivered it up to the Inclemencies of Sun and Winters. In the Deserts of the West, still today, there are Tattered Ruins of that Map, inhabited by Animals and Beggars; in all the Land there is no other Relic of the Disciplines of Geography.

—Suárez Miranda, Viajes de varones prudentes, Libro IV, Cap. XLV, Lérida, 1658

Jorge Luis Borges, Collected Fictions, translated by Andrew Hurley.

Ecology and evolution deal with objects — individuals — that are absurdly complicated, in numbers that range from a handful to \(10^{30}\). No model of a population is a faithful description of its parts.

One cannot simultaneously maximise generality, realism and precision, so model building is the choice of which one to sacrifice (Levins 1966). In this course we mostly sacrifice realism and precision: we look for the simplest dynamical rule compatible with a biological mechanism, and we ask what it predicts qualitatively and, when possible, quantitatively.

Generality vs realism?

What we will write, and what it leaves out

Most of this course is about equations of the form

\[ \frac{dn_i}{dt} = f_i(\vec n, \vec E) , \qquad \frac{dE_k}{dt} = g_k(\vec n, \vec E) . \]

\(n_i\) is the abundance of population \(i\). \(\vec E\) is the environment (resources, temperature, pH, …), which in general is itself changed by the populations: food is eaten. The same form also describes the structure within a population (genotypes, phenotypes), and that is how evolution enters.

The form looks general, but it hides several assumptions. Most of them are dropped somewhere in the course.

  • First order in time. Not a real restriction: a second-order equation is a system of two first-order ones.
  • Well mixed. There is no space: where an individual sits does not matter. Space comes back towards the end of the course.
  • Continuous numbers and continuous time. Individuals are discrete and are born at discrete times. This is a good approximation for large populations that reproduce all the time. It fails for small populations, and for populations that reproduce once a year, like annual plants. Discrete generations are in L1; discrete individuals in L2.
  • Deterministic. The initial condition fixes the whole trajectory. Demographic noise is in L2, random mutations in L3.
  • Autonomous. There is no explicit time dependence: no seasons, no fluctuating environment. A changing environment can be put back through \(\vec E\), if we know how to write its dynamics.
  • Well-defined types. All individuals of type \(i\) are equivalent, with the same birth and death rates. Paint half of them red and half blue, and the two halves behave the same. What a species is, and whether this holds, is not obvious in many contexts. Mutations create new types (L3).
